## Abstract

Communication strategies (CSs) have long been conceptualized as compensatory devices that second and foreign language speakers deploy to overcome linguistic deficits and repair communication breakdowns. This compensatory framing, most influentially articulated in Dörnyei and Scott's (1997) taxonomy, has shaped decades of research in English as a Foreign Language (EFL) classrooms. The present paper argues that this framing is insufficient for understanding how experienced teachers respond to learner questions in advanced EFL settings, where communication rarely breaks down and where teacher responses are carefully constructed interactional achievements rather than remedial repairs. Drawing on conversation analysis (CA) and on evidence from advanced university-level EFL classrooms, the paper reconceptualizes teacher communication strategies as interactional and pedagogical resources that manage discourse, scaffold understanding, and co-construct meaning with learners. It proposes an integrated framework that combines Dörnyei and Scott's (1997) taxonomy with CA's sequence organization (turn-taking, repair, and topic management) to analyze teacher responses to learner questions. The paper reviews the relevant literature, identifies a persistent gap between compensatory and interactional accounts, articulates the framework's components and analytic procedure, and discusses pedagogical, methodological, and research implications. It concludes with directions for future research that can test and refine the framework across proficiency levels, instructional contexts, and languages.

**Keywords:** communication strategies; conversation analysis; EFL classroom interaction; teacher responses; learner questions; code-switching; interactional resources

## 1. Introduction

Classroom interaction is widely recognized as a primary engine of second and foreign language development (Allwright, 1984; Antón, 1999; Hall, 2022). Within this interaction, the exchange that begins with a learner's question and unfolds through a teacher's response is a particularly consequential moment: it is here that gaps in comprehension are surfaced, conceptual understanding is negotiated, and the trajectory of classroom discourse is set (Chin & Osborne, 2008; Jiang, 2014; Redfield & Rousseau, 1981). Despite the centrality of this exchange, the literature on communication strategies (CSs) has paid comparatively little attention to how teachers specifically deploy such strategies when responding to learner questions. Most studies have either catalogued the strategies used by learners to compensate for linguistic shortfalls (e.g., Hua et al., 2012; Nakatani, 2010; Rahman & Isroyana, 2021) or have treated teacher strategies as a mirror image of the same compensatory process (e.g., Bhatti et al., 2018; Bukit et al., 2023; Pangaribuan et al., 2020). The implicit assumption has been that communication strategies are, by definition, devices for coping with trouble.

This paper challenges that assumption. It argues that in advanced EFL classrooms, where learners possess substantial linguistic resources and where instruction is organized around abstract academic discourse, teacher communication strategies function less as compensatory repairs and more as interactional resources that manage discourse, scaffold comprehension, and foster collaborative meaning-making. The argument is not that compensatory strategies never occur; rather, it is that the compensatory framing has obscured a large and pedagogically significant class of strategy use that is better understood through the lens of conversation analysis (CA). By integrating Dörnyei and Scott's (1997) influential taxonomy with CA's account of sequence organization, the paper proposes a framework for analyzing teacher responses to learner questions that captures both what strategies are used and how they are interactionally deployed.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it offers a critical review of the CS literature that distinguishes compensatory from interactional uses and identifies the conceptual gap between them. Second, it proposes an integrated analytic framework—the Interactional Communication Strategy Framework (ICSF)—that combines taxonomy and CA. Third, it derives pedagogical, methodological, and research implications, including an agenda for testing the framework across contexts. The paper is conceptual in nature: it does not report new empirical data but synthesizes and reinterprets existing evidence, including evidence from advanced university-level EFL classrooms, to advance a theoretical position that can guide future empirical work.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the relevant literature on communication strategies, teacher responses to student questions, and conversation analysis. Section 3 identifies the gap that motivates the reconceptualization. Section 4 presents the ICSF. Section 5 discusses implications. Section 6 acknowledges limitations and proposes directions for future research. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 Communication Strategies: Definitions and Taxonomies

The concept of communication strategies (CSs) emerged from the study of interlanguage and second language use. Corder (1975) and Tarone (1980, 1981) defined CSs as techniques that speakers employ to overcome difficulties in conveying or comprehending messages. Faerch and Kasper (1983) distinguished between reduction strategies (simplifying the message) and achievement strategies (finding alternative means of expression). Bialystok (1990) emphasized the psychological processes underlying strategy selection, while Dörnyei (1995) foregrounded the teachability of strategic competence. Paribakht (1985) and Canale and Swain (1980) situated CSs within broader models of communicative competence.

The most influential taxonomy for EFL classroom research remains that of Dörnyei and Scott (1997), who classified CSs into three categories: direct strategies (resource-deficit, own-performance, and other-performance related), indirect strategies (processing time pressure, own-performance, and other-performance related), and interactional strategies (resource-deficit, own-performance, and other-performance related). Direct strategies include message abandonment, circumlocution, approximation, code-switching, self-repair, and other repair. Indirect strategies include repetition, fillers, verbal strategy markers, and feigning understanding. Interactional strategies include appeals for help, accuracy and comprehension checks, asking for repetition or clarification, guessing, expressing non-understanding, interpretive summary, and responses. This taxonomy has been widely adopted in EFL classroom studies because it provides a systematic and exhaustive inventory (Afzali & Kianpoor, 2020; Al-Gharaibeh & Al-Jamal, 2016; Cahyani et al., 2023; Yaghoubi Notash & Karafkan, 2015).

However, the taxonomy's organizing logic is fundamentally compensatory: strategies are grouped according to the type of communication problem they address (resource deficit, performance problem, time pressure). This problem-management orientation is productive for describing learner behavior in tasks where linguistic resources are limited, but it becomes less adequate when applied to expert teachers in advanced classrooms, where the "problem" is often not a linguistic gap but an interactional or pedagogical one. The taxonomy can describe *what* a teacher does (e.g., code-switches, self-repairs, asks for confirmation) but not *why* it is interactionally deployed or *how* it is sequentially organized.

### 2.2 Communication Strategies in EFL Classrooms

A large body of research has examined CS use in EFL classrooms. Studies of learners have shown that proficiency level shapes strategy selection, with clarification requests, code-switching, approximation, and appeals for assistance among the most frequent (Al Alawi, 2016; Dewi et al., 2018; Hua et al., 2012; Nakatani, 2010; Namaziandost & Imani, 2020; Rahman & Isroyana, 2021; Uztosun & Erten, 2014). Studies of teachers have documented the frequency and functions of teacher CS use (Bhatti et al., 2018; Bukit et al., 2023; Cahyani et al., 2018, 2023; Ding et al., 2022; Oktaviani & Misgiyati, 2018; Pangaribuan et al., 2020; Puspawati, 2018; Yao, 2011). Comparative studies have highlighted differences between teachers and learners in strategy selection (Afzali & Kianpoor, 2020; Al-Gharaibeh & Al-Jamal, 2016; Cervantes & Rodriguez, 2012; Dörnyei & Scott, 1997; Essien et al., 2022; Nourdad & Hosseini, 2022; Santos et al., 2016).

Code-switching has emerged as a particularly important and contested strategy. While some traditions have treated L1 use in the classroom with suspicion (Yao, 2011), a substantial literature now documents its multifunctionality: code-switching serves pedagogical functions (clarifying complex content, managing discourse, maintaining instructional momentum), affective functions (building rapport, reducing anxiety), and rhetorical functions (emphasizing key terms, contrasting personalization and objectification) (Ariffin & Rafik-Galea, 2009; Bhatti et al., 2018; Cahyani et al., 2018; Canagarajah, 1995; Eldridge, 1996; Jingxia, 2010; Metila, 2009; Muñoz & Mora, 2006; Sert, 2005; Üstünel & Seedhouse, 2005). Recent systematic reviews confirm the field's growing interest in code-switching and translanguaging as pedagogical resources rather than deficits (Alsalami, 2025; Alaoui Mrani & Azmi, 2025; Huang & Chalmers, 2023). These developments suggest a broader shift in the field: from viewing L1 use as a failure to be minimized to viewing it as a resource to be strategically deployed.

### 2.3 Teacher Responses to Learner Questions

The specific phenomenon of teacher responses to learner questions has been studied from several angles. Research on teacher questioning has established that question type and questioning behavior shape student participation and learning (Dillon, 1988; Jiang, 2014; Nystrand et al., 2003; Redfield & Rousseau, 1981). Studies of classroom discourse have shown that teacher responses to student questions are interactionally complex: they may clarify, extend, redirect, or evaluate, and they often involve multiple turns (Can Daşkın, 2015; Yuksel, 2014). Chin and Osborne (2008) argued that student questions are a potential resource for teaching and learning, and Dillon (1988) noted the remedial status often assigned to student questioning. More recent work has examined how teachers shape learner contributions (Can Daşkın, 2015) and how teacher-student exchanges are sequentially organized (Cancino, 2015; Ko, 2009; Maroni et al., 2008).

However, the CS literature and the teacher-questioning literature have developed largely in parallel. CS studies rarely analyze the sequential organization of teacher responses, while CA studies of classroom discourse rarely use CS taxonomies to categorize the strategies embedded in those responses. The result is a gap: we know which strategies teachers use, and we know how classroom sequences are organized, but we have limited integrated accounts of how specific strategies are deployed within the sequential unfolding of a response to a learner question.

### 2.4 Conversation Analysis and Classroom Discourse

Conversation analysis (CA) provides the methodological and theoretical tools to address this gap. CA examines how talk is organized sequentially in naturally occurring interaction, focusing on turn-taking, sequence organization, and repair (Sacks, Schegloff, & Jefferson, 1974; Schegloff, Jefferson, & Sacks, 1977). In institutional settings such as classrooms, CA has been used to explicate the interactional architecture of language teaching (Seedhouse, 2004), to analyze repair as a role-defining mechanism (Liebscher & Dailey-O'Cain, 2003; McHoul, 1990), and to examine how teachers shape learner contributions (Can Daşkın, 2015; Cancino, 2015).

CA's relevance to CS research is substantial. Where CS taxonomies categorize isolated strategy types, CA reveals how strategies are *sequentially deployed*—how a teacher's self-repair, code-switch, or clarification request is positioned within a turn, how it relates to the learner's prior question, and how it projects subsequent learner uptake. CA also provides concepts for analyzing strategies that taxonomies handle poorly: topic management (how a response develops, shifts, or closes a topic; Button & Casey, 1984, 1985, 1988; Dolce & van Compernolle, 2020; Gan et al., 2009; Stokoe, 2000), turn-taking (Ford, 2013; Ingram & Elliott, 2014; Maroni et al., 2008), and the preference for self-correction (Schegloff et al., 1977). Kasper and Wagner (2014) documented CA's growing contribution to applied linguistics, and Borg (2003) highlighted the importance of examining teacher cognition in action. Wong and Waring (2010) demonstrated how CA can inform second language pedagogy.

### 2.5 The Compensatory Bias in CS Research

A critical reading of the CS literature reveals a systematic bias: strategies are predominantly studied as responses to *trouble*. Studies of learners focus on how they cope with lexical gaps (Hua et al., 2012; Nakatani, 2010). Studies of teachers focus on how they manage breakdowns (Essien et al., 2022; Fotovatnia & Dorri, 2013; Madar, 2021). Even studies that document strategy frequency often interpret the absence of a strategy as the absence of trouble (e.g., the rarity of mime or approximation in advanced classrooms is read as evidence of high proficiency). This framing is not wrong, but it is incomplete: it cannot account for strategy use that is not triggered by trouble, such as a teacher's strategic code-switch to build rapport, or a deliberate self-repair to model academic precision.

Recent work has begun to move beyond compensation. Studies of code-switching have documented affective and rhetorical functions that are not trouble-driven (Ariffin & Rafik-Galea, 2009; Canagarajah, 1995; Sert, 2005). Studies of self-repair have shown that it can serve pedagogical rather than corrective purposes (Liebscher & Dailey-O'Cain, 2003; Simpson et al., 2013). Studies of clarification requests and confirmation checks have framed them as participation-eliciting devices rather than comprehension checks alone (Can Daşkın, 2015; Cervantes & Rodriguez, 2012; Santos et al., 2016). These findings point toward a richer conception of CSs as interactional resources, but they remain fragmented and have not been integrated into a coherent framework.

## 3. The Gap: From Compensation to Interaction

The gap that motivates this paper can be stated as follows. The CS literature, anchored in Dörnyei and Scott's (1997) taxonomy, conceptualizes strategies as problem-management devices. The CA literature conceptualizes classroom talk as sequentially organized interaction. The two literatures address the same phenomenon—how teachers and learners manage meaning in the classroom—but from incompatible starting points. CS research asks "which strategy was used and how often?" CA research asks "how is this turn positioned and what does it accomplish sequentially?" Neither question is sufficient alone.

This gap has practical consequences. Teacher training programs that teach CSs as a list of compensatory techniques (e.g., "use code-switching when students don't understand") miss the interactional dimension: the same strategy can clarify, redirect, build rapport, or model precision depending on its sequential position and the teacher's pedagogical goal. Conversely, CA-informed teacher education that ignores CS taxonomies lacks a vocabulary for naming the specific resources teachers deploy. An integrated framework would allow teacher educators to say, for example, "this teacher's code-switch is functioning as a topic-shift device in the third turn of a response sequence," combining the CS label with the CA analysis.

The gap is particularly acute for the phenomenon of teacher responses to learner questions. Learner questions are not, in advanced classrooms, primarily signs of linguistic trouble; they are often signs of intellectual engagement (Chin & Osborne, 2008; Song, 2016). Teacher responses to such questions are correspondingly not primarily compensatory; they are pedagogical and interactional achievements. A framework that can only describe them as "strategy use" or "sequence organization" but not both is inadequate.

## 4. The Interactional Communication Strategy Framework (ICSF)

### 4.1 Overview

The ICSF integrates two analytic traditions. From Dörnyei and Scott (1997), it takes the inventory of communication strategies and their categorization. From CA, it takes the concepts of turn-taking, sequence organization, repair, and topic management. The integration is achieved by treating each strategy not as a standalone device but as an action performed within a sequentially organized response. The unit of analysis is the *teacher response to a learner question*, understood as a multi-turn sequence in which the teacher deploys one or more strategies.

The framework has four components:

1. **Strategy inventory (the "what").** The strategies are drawn from Dörnyei and Scott's (1997) taxonomy, but are reorganized by interactional function rather than by problem type. Three interactional functions are proposed: (a) *managing understanding* (clarifying, confirming, repairing), (b) *managing discourse* (developing, shifting, or closing a topic; maintaining fluency), and (c) *managing affect and relationships* (building rapport, reducing anxiety, personalizing instruction).

2. **Sequence position (the "where").** Each strategy is located within the response sequence: in the initial answer, in a clarification or elaboration, in a repair, or in a closing move. The position determines what the strategy can accomplish.

3. **Co-occurrence pattern (the "how combined").** Strategies rarely occur in isolation. The framework documents which strategies co-occur within a single response and how their combination produces effects (e.g., code-switching followed by self-repair to clarify and then confirm understanding).

4. **Interactional outcome (the "so what").** The framework records what the strategy or combination achieves: comprehension is reached, the topic is developed, the learner participates, or the discourse is closed.

### 4.2 Analytic Procedure

The ICSF is applied in four steps:

1. **Identify the trigger.** Locate a learner question that initiates a response sequence.
2. **Transcribe.** Transcribe the ensuing teacher response (and any learner uptake) using CA conventions (Jefferson, 2008).
3. **Code.** Identify each strategy in the response using the Dörnyei and Scott (1997) inventory, and assign it to one of the three interactional functions, a sequence position, and a co-occurrence pattern.
4. **Interpret.** Analyze how the coded strategies accomplish interactional work within the sequence, drawing on CA concepts (turn-taking, repair, topic management).

This procedure preserves the descriptive power of the CS taxonomy while adding the sequential and interactional analysis that CA provides. It also makes the framework applicable to both quantitative (frequency, co-occurrence) and qualitative (sequential, functional) research questions.

### 4.3 Reorganizing the Taxonomy by Interactional Function

Table 1 illustrates how Dörnyei and Scott's (1997) strategies can be reorganized under the three interactional functions. The reorganization is not a replacement of the original taxonomy but a reinterpretation: the same strategy can serve different functions depending on context, and the framework allows for multiple functions.

| Interactional function | Strategies (from Dörnyei & Scott, 1997) |
|---|---|
| Managing understanding | Self-repair, other repair, clarification requests, comprehension checks, confirmation checks, asking for repetition, interpretive summary, repetition |
| Managing discourse | Code-switching (topic shift, obviating difficulty), circumlocution, restructuring, fillers, message reduction, topic management moves |
| Managing affect and relationships | Code-switching (affective, personalization), humor, verbal strategy markers, feigning understanding |

**Table 1.** Reorganization of Dörnyei and Scott's (1997) strategies by interactional function (ICSF).

The reorganization highlights a key insight: code-switching, the most frequent strategy in advanced EFL classrooms, is not a single strategy but a multifunctional resource that can manage understanding, discourse, and affect simultaneously (Canagarajah, 1995; Sert, 2005; Üstünel & Seedhouse, 2005). Similarly, self-repair, often read as disfluency, can be reinterpreted as a resource for maintaining conceptual accuracy and modeling academic rigor (Liebscher & Dailey-O'Cain, 2003; Simpson et al., 2013).

### 4.4 Illustrative Application

Consider a teacher responding to a learner's question about an abstract theoretical concept. The teacher's response might proceed as follows: an initial answer in English (managing understanding), a code-switch to the L1 to clarify the concept's difficulty (managing understanding and discourse), a self-repair to refine the English formulation (managing understanding), a confirmation check (managing understanding), and a code-switched humorous aside (managing affect). Under the compensatory framing, this response would be decomposed into a list of strategies and their frequencies. Under the ICSF, it is analyzed as a sequentially organized achievement in which strategies are layered to accomplish understanding, discourse development, and rapport simultaneously. The ICSF thus explains not only what the teacher did but how the response was interactionally built.

## 5. Implications

### 5.1 Pedagogical Implications

The ICSF has several implications for teacher education and classroom practice. First, it suggests that teacher training should move beyond presenting CSs as a checklist of compensatory techniques. Instead, training should develop teachers' awareness of how strategies function interactionally and sequentially. Teachers should be encouraged to view learner questions not as interruptions but as opportunities for co-constructive dialogue, in which strategies such as confirmation, repetition, and code-switching promote collaborative engagement and reduce learner anxiety (Ariffin & Rafik-Galea, 2009; Jingxia, 2010; Sert, 2005).

Second, the framework implies that the same strategy can be taught for multiple purposes. Code-switching, for example, can be taught not only as a clarification device but as a tool for discourse management and affective support. Self-repair can be taught as a model of academic precision rather than as a sign of hesitation. This multi-purpose view aligns with the concept of strategic competence (Canale & Swain, 1980; Paribakht, 1985) and with the view that strategic flexibility is a key component of communicative competence.

Third, the framework suggests that classroom materials and evaluation practices should reflect authentic interactional patterns. Textbook developers could incorporate examples of how teachers and students use strategies to manage discourse, and classroom observation instruments could be revised to assess interactional strategy use rather than mere frequency.

### 5.2 Methodological Implications

The ICSF implies a methodological shift. Rather than counting strategy occurrences in isolation, researchers should analyze strategies within their sequential context. This requires CA-informed transcription and analysis, but it does not require abandoning quantitative methods: co-occurrence patterns, sequence positions, and functional distributions can be quantified. The framework thus bridges qualitative and quantitative traditions and can accommodate both case studies and larger-scale comparative designs.

The framework also suggests that future studies should analyze the *response* as the unit of analysis rather than the individual strategy. This shift would allow researchers to ask new questions: How many strategies are typically combined in a response? Which combinations are most effective for promoting understanding? How does the sequential organization of a response vary with learner proficiency or instructional context?

### 5.3 Research Implications

The ICSF opens several research avenues. Studies could test whether the three interactional functions (managing understanding, discourse, and affect) are empirically distinguishable and whether they predict learner outcomes. Comparative studies could examine how the framework applies across proficiency levels, where compensatory strategies may be more prevalent (Nakatani, 2006; Rabab'ah, 2002; Santos et al., 2016). Cross-linguistic studies could test whether the framework's functions are universal or language-specific. And studies could examine how the framework informs teacher cognition: whether teachers who are aware of interactional functions deploy strategies more effectively (Borg, 2003).

## 6. Limitations and Directions for Future Research

The ICSF is a conceptual proposal and has several limitations. First, it has not yet been empirically validated; the evidence cited is drawn from existing studies and from advanced university-level EFL classrooms, and its applicability to other contexts remains to be tested. Second, the reorganization of Dörnyei and Scott's (1997) taxonomy by interactional function is interpretive: the same strategy can serve multiple functions, and coding decisions require analytic judgment. Third, the framework assumes that CA concepts are applicable to the classroom context, which, while well supported (Seedhouse, 2004; Wong & Waring, 2010), requires training and expertise.

Future research should: (1) apply the ICSF to diverse EFL contexts (private language schools, online platforms, mixed-proficiency classrooms) to test its generalizability; (2) conduct comparative studies across teacher experience levels and genders; (3) incorporate the learner's perspective through stimulated recall and post-lesson surveys (as suggested by the limitations of teacher-focused studies); (4) develop and validate a coding scheme based on the ICSF; and (5) examine whether ICSF-informed teacher training produces measurable changes in classroom interaction and learner outcomes.

---

## 7. Conclusion

This paper has argued that the compensatory framing of communication strategies, while productive for describing learner behavior in resource-limited tasks, is insufficient for understanding teacher responses to learner questions in advanced EFL classrooms. By integrating Dörnyei and Scott's (1997) taxonomy with conversation analysis, the proposed Interactional Communication Strategy Framework (ICSF) reconceptualizes teacher communication strategies as interactional and pedagogical resources that manage understanding, discourse, and affect within sequentially organized responses. The framework preserves the descriptive power of existing taxonomies while adding the sequential and functional analysis needed to explain how teacher responses are interactionally built. It offers pedagogical implications for teacher training, methodological implications for classroom research, and a research agenda for testing and refining the framework across contexts. In doing so, it aims to bridge two traditions—CS research and CA—that have addressed the same classroom phenomena from incompatible starting points, and to advance a conception of teacher communication that recognizes its strategic, interactional, and pedagogical dimensions.
